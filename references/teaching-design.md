# Teaching Design

## Design from learning outcomes

Write outcomes as observable viewer actions, such as explain a mechanism, distinguish two ideas, predict a result, interpret a case, or apply a rule to a new example. Avoid relying only on verbs such as `know` or `understand` unless paired with observable evidence.

For each outcome, map:

| Learning outcome | Prerequisite | Content/claim | Teaching move | Visual/audio treatment | Viewer action/evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Check that every essential segment serves an outcome, every outcome receives adequate explanation and practice, and assessment asks for the intended action at an appropriate difficulty. Remove attractive material that distracts from the purpose or label it as enrichment.

## Choose methods by fit

Treat methods as design options, not a checklist. Select a small coherent set based on the learning outcome, learner background, subject matter, time, and medium. Explain why a method fits, what the viewer will do, and what evidence will show it worked. Distinguish established evidence from plausible design rationale; do not promise learning gains from a method name alone.

| Method | Useful when | Video implementation | Check / caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Socratic questioning | Expose assumptions, surface misconceptions, or guide reasoning | Ask a focused question, pause visibly, offer likely response paths, then reason through the answer | Avoid performative questions with no thinking time or feedback |
| Problem-oriented learning | Learners need to use knowledge to resolve a meaningful problem | Introduce a problem before explanation; reveal information as needed; return to the problem | Keep the problem aligned to outcomes and avoid unnecessary case complexity |
| First-principles derivation | A mechanism or conclusion can be built from explicit premises | State premises, show each inference, identify evidence and limits | Do not imply complex phenomena have only one cause or skip supporting evidence |
| Longitudinal/historical narrative | Development over time explains the current state | Select turning points, show causal links and changes in evidence or interpretation | Avoid a timeline that lists dates without explaining why change occurred |
| Cross-sectional comparison | Distinguishing parallel concepts, products, models, or competitors matters | Compare on shared dimensions in a side-by-side table or repeated visual frame | Use fair, relevant dimensions; distinguish facts from judgments; avoid cherry-picking |
| Case-based teaching | Transfer to realistic decisions or interpretation matters | Present a case, ask for a prediction/choice, reveal evidence, explain decision | State what the case illustrates and what it cannot establish |
| Elaboration and self-explanation | Learners need to connect new ideas to prior knowledge or justify steps | Prompt `why/how`, ask for a prediction, then model a concise explanation | Keep prompts answerable and provide feedback; avoid adding cognitive load without purpose |
| Concept maps and knowledge organization | Relationships, hierarchy, feedback, or causal structure are central | Build a map progressively; label links with meaningful relations | Every connector should mean something; map should aid reasoning rather than decorate |
| Interleaved practice | Learners must discriminate among related categories or strategies | Alternate short cases or examples after initial instruction; ask which rule applies | Usually useful after basic distinctions are introduced; do not confuse with random topic switching |
| Spacing and retrieval | Long-term retention matters across episodes or sessions | Revisit a prior idea after a delay; ask for recall before showing the answer | Schedule repeated ideas intentionally and track where they recur |
| Varied-context recurrence | Transfer across situations matters | Reintroduce the same concept in a new case, scale, representation, or decision | Preserve the invariant idea while making the changed context visible |
| Analogy/metaphor | An abstract relation needs a familiar starting point | Map source to target explicitly; return from analogy to the real mechanism | State where the analogy breaks; never let imagery replace the accurate explanation |
| Retrieval question and delayed answer | Viewer should retrieve, reflect, or anticipate a later lesson | End with a clear prompt and response pause; log the answer/feedback episode | Maintain a question-to-answer ledger; do not leave a promised answer unfulfilled |

## Use audiovisual design to teach

- Decide whether a presenter, dialogue, narration, characters, animation, screen recording, or combination helps the goal. Two presenters need distinct instructional functions (for example, guide and novice, competing hypotheses, or questioner and explainer); do not add dialogue solely for variety.
- Define recurring persona traits operationally: role, audience relationship, knowledge level, vocabulary, pace, humor boundary, visual identifiers, gestures, and what the character should not claim. Separate persistent series traits from episode-specific behavior.
- Plan opening and ending as instructional elements. The opening should establish a relevant question/problem or context quickly. Recurring branding should not delay the lesson. The ending may retrieve, summarize, apply, preview, or assign reflection; use only components that serve the goal.
- Storyboard complex information at the same time as narration. Give every diagram a reading order, labels, and explicit relation to what is spoken. Plan for vertical and horizontal compositions rather than assuming automatic cropping preserves meaning.
- Use pauses and on-screen prompts when viewers are expected to think. Allow enough time for the requested action and provide a reveal or explanation.

## Multi-episode learning architecture

For series/ongoing work, keep a concept recurrence ledger: concept, first introduction, prerequisite status, later retrieval, varied contexts, misconceptions to revisit, and answer episode for any open question. Plan spacing intentionally instead of repeating the same example immediately. Track character/visual continuity and terminology across episodes, while recording intentional changes.

## Evidence and claims

For factual or scientific teaching, tie substantive claims to dated, traceable sources with scope and caveats. Distinguish consensus, contested interpretation, hypothesis, and illustrative simplification. The creator or designated subject expert owns final approval of consequential claims. Recheck changeable facts before release.
